Beginner guide

The handful of ideas you need to read this site, and to stay safe while you learn.

Rule number one: never invest money you can't afford to lose. Keep an emergency fund first, and pay off high-interest debt. Stock prices can drop 30% or more and take years to recover.

What is a stock?

A stock (also called a share) is a tiny piece of ownership in a company. If you own one share of a company with a billion shares, you own one-billionth of it. The price changes every second the market is open, as people trade shares based on what they think the company is worth. Some companies also pay part of their profits to shareholders as dividends.

What is an ETF?

An ETF (exchange-traded fund) is a basket of many investments that trades like a single stock. SPY and VOO, for example, each hold all ~500 companies in the S&P 500. VTI holds almost every US public company. Owning one share of an ETF spreads your money across all of them at once.

What is the S&P 500?

A list of about 500 of the largest US companies. Its overall movement is used as “the market”. When this site says a stock is “beating the market”, it means it has done better than the S&P 500 over that time.

What is a moving average?

The average closing price over the last N trading days, recalculated every day so it “moves” along. A 50-day average shows the medium-term direction; a 200-day average shows the long-term direction. A price above its averages has generally been rising. It's a description of the past, not a forecast.

What is volatility?

How much a price jumps around. A stock with 15% yearly volatility is relatively calm; 50% is very jumpy. Higher volatility means bigger swings in both directions, and more chance of a painful drop right when you need the money.

What is a drawdown?

The fall from a high point to a later low. If a stock went from $100 to $70, that's a 30% drawdown. Note the unfair math: after a 30% drop, you need a 43% gain just to get back to even.

Why diversification matters

Any single company can run into trouble: a failed product, a scandal, a new competitor. Spreading money across many companies and industries means one bad surprise can't sink you. That's why the scores on this site should never be a reason to put a lot of money into one stock.

Why index funds are popular with beginners

Low-cost index funds (like the S&P 500 or total-market funds) give you instant diversification for a very small yearly fee. Research has repeatedly found that most professional stock pickers fail to beat a plain index fund over long periods, after fees. Many experienced investors keep most of their money in index funds for this reason.

Practice with paper trading first

Paper trading means practicing with pretend money. Many brokers, including Alpaca, offer free paper accounts that work like the real thing. Try your ideas there for a few months. You'll learn how it feels to watch a position fall, and whether your approach actually works, without risking a cent.

Other basics worth knowing

  • Time horizon: money you need within a few years usually doesn't belong in stocks.
  • Fees and taxes eat into returns, and frequent trading increases both.
  • Emotions are the biggest risk. Fear and excitement push people to make changes at the worst moments.
  • Scams: be suspicious of anyone promising certain or fast returns, or pushing you to act quickly.
  • Get help: a licensed, fee-only financial adviser can give advice about your situation. This site can't.
